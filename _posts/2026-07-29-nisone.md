If you have a single observation from a normal distribution $$X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$$, where both $$\mu$$ and $$\sigma^2$$ are unknown, did you know you can still get a valid, finite-length confidence interval for $$\mu$$? This sounded wild to me when I first heard it. To build a confidence interval you need some sense of the spread of the data. So how do you get that from a single number?

Turns out you get it by comparing $$X$$ to some fixed number $$A$$, chosen ahead of time. You can then construct intervals of the form

$$
\frac{X + A}{2} \pm \eta |X - A|,
$$

where $$\eta$$ is big enough to control the error probability.

This interval just *stinks* of Bayesianism. You are *forced* to plug in a *prior* guess for the mean ($$A$$), and the interval performs better when $$A$$ is close to $$\mu$$. But no formal Bayesian justification for it has ever been produced.

Until now. In my manuscript (Gerard, 2026), I lay out two separate Bayesian constructions. First, I derive priors whose credible intervals for $$\mu$$ are approximate confidence intervals (as the confidence level approaches 1). Second, I get confidence intervals by inverting frequentist tests that use a Bayes factor (under specific priors) as the test statistic. That second approach generalizes easily to $$n \geq 2$$, and it beats the Student $$t$$-interval in parts of the parameter space (when $$A$$ is relatively close to $$\mu$$).

Are these methods useful? Well, sometimes datasets of real scientific interest are tiny. Humanity has only ever observed three confirmed interstellar objects passing through our solar system, and I use that $$n = 3$$ dataset to estimate the mean hyperbolic excess velocity of interstellar objects.

Check out the paper:

**Gerard, D.** (2026). Constructing and extending *n* = 1 Bayesian confidence intervals for location parameters in location-scale families. *arXiv Preprint*. [doi:10.48550/arXiv.2607.25007](https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2607.25007)
